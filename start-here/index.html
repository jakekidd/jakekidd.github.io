This is a blog about the future of technology, and what could go wrong.

There are a lot of things that can go wrong with technology, many of which already have. In the last few years we’ve heard some frankly insane promises, but also seen a number of wildly staggering breakthroughs, some of which, it’s being argued, were made by the technology itself.

Coverage can’t keep up with the level of precision required to describe tech well enough that anybody walks away with a better understanding. What we’re left with is a narrative that’s starkly polarizing, arguing either that technology will surely bring our salvation, or that it will surely bring our doom. No headline could be more sensational (despite the many other real problems in the world), which leaves you wondering how much of it is merely a thinly veiled marketing tactic, and what other ways this could go. What could go wrong?

People have wanted a machine that solves problems for a long time. In the 1680s Leibniz imagined one that would settle any argument: two people would sit down together, in some kind of drawing room I’d imagine, over tea, and simply say, “Let us calculate.”1 In 1928 David Hilbert asked whether a mechanical procedure could decide every mathematical statement,2 and in 1936 Alan Turing proved that none can.3 We’re asking it again anyway. The question on everyone’s mind is whether machines can solve every problem.

Our technology is becoming indistinguishable from magic.4 But it’s not magic. This time the man behind the curtain isn’t a humbug, he’s just a machine, and machines can be understood. And if you’re skeptical, curious, or confused, and would rather understand the thing than just be told how to feel about it, this blog is for you.

What could go wrong? Let us calculate.
